# Who are they

A character's sheet can hold who a person is: their identity, how they think and how they take things, their body and mind, and what formed them. It is there to help you write people with nuance, and nothing more:

- **Every field is optional.** Nothing counts toward a sheet being complete, and nothing nudges you to fill it. An empty field is a word in the Add row.
- **Nothing here is a flaw.** A disability, an illness, a gender or a history is not a fault of character; Flaws stays what it is.
- **Your words.** Every field is free text with suggestions. Identity-first ("autistic", "Deaf") or person-first ("has epilepsy") is yours to choose.
- **Nothing is scored.** No page counts representation or says whether a book passes anything.

Open a character and choose **Who are they** beside Overview.

## Identity

Gender, how they present, sex and pronouns are four different things, and the sheet keeps them apart: androgynous is mostly how someone presents, and a woman, a man or a non-binary person can present androgynously. Gender is asked beside pronouns in the character form too.

The gender suggestions include terms that belong to a particular people (two-spirit, hijra, fa'afafine, muxe, burrnesha), each shown with where it comes from, because each is a word of its own and not another way of saying non-binary. If you use one, it is worth reading about it from the people it belongs to. An invented world's genders are typed as you name them.

Age, orientation, languages, heritage, faith, family and money and class sit beside them.

## How they think and feel

**How they think** is in words ("in pictures", "out loud", "in maps and bearings"). Beside it are the scales that shape a voice in the interview: **Thinks** says how someone thinks, not how well (abstractly, quickly, practically, concretely, unhurriedly). **Sensitivity** joins temperament: temperament is how fast they boil, sensitivity is what reaches them.

**How they take things** says what gets under their skin, how personally they take things, how it shows, how they cope and how long they hold on.

## Body and mind

One entry for each thing worth knowing: moving, senses, communicating, long-term health and pain, neurodivergence, mental health, or something else. Name it in your words, then say, as much as you want:

- **how it shapes their days**: what they plan around, what it costs, what they are good at because of it;
- **what it does to them**: in how they think, what they do, how they are with people (for anxiety: the thought that loops at 3 a.m., the plans made around exits, the friend who stopped being invited);
- **how it shows on the page**: what a reader notices;
- **what they'd want understood**;
- **who knows**: everyone, some (name them), or only them; and **the scene where the reader learns it**;
- **research**: Compendium entries that inform it.

It is not a medical record: there is no severity scale, symptom list or treatment field.

## What formed them

Formative experiences: what happened, before or during the story, what it did to them and how it shows. Adversity is broader than trauma (poverty, displacement, a long illness in the family, a public failure), and a good thing belongs here too. Tick **the wound behind their arc** for the one the story tests; the Arc journey reads the arc in one line from it: formed by, believes, wants, needs, against, learns.

**Content notes** are for you only. An entry with them keeps what happened folded until you open it, so you never meet it unprepared on a sheet opened for something else. They are never sent to a model.

## The three questions

Overview opens on Debra Dixon's **Goal, Motivation and Conflict**: what do they want, why, and what stands in the way, with the stakes many writers add (what if they don't get it). **Needs** and **Believes** (the lie the story tests) follow. Plan › The essentials asks the same questions.

## At the scene

The scene panel shows, in your own words, how bodies and minds show in that scene: the point of view's senses, movement or mental health, and each other person on the page. It is there while you write, which is where a slip is easiest to catch.

## Changing pronouns or a name

Change them in the character form and **Review the manuscript** opens: every sentence that should follow, under its scene, the changed words marked and what it was beneath. Tick what should change; **Apply** saves a version of the story first, then makes every ticked sentence and the new pronouns or name one change, undone with one Undo. A scene written in since is left alone. **Skip** changes only the field. The sheet's ⋯ › **Review pronouns…** finds slips after their name at any time.

**Quick, on this machine** finds each pronoun and the verb it governs ("she was" becomes "they were"), and marks each sentence **Sure** or **Unsure**. It cannot tell for certain who a pronoun means, so it will miss:

- two people with the same old pronouns in one scene, where a pronoun isn't next to the right name (it marks these unsure rather than guess);
- a pronoun whose person was named paragraphs earlier;
- gendered words (the woman, Mrs Holt, ma'am): listed for you, with no rewrite;
- other people talking about them in dialogue: listed as unsure;
- singular or plural "they" when moving away from they/them;
- long sentences the small language model parses wrongly, a verb far from its subject;
- pronoun sets other than she, he, they, xe, ze and it.

In Studio, **Careful, with the Assistant** reads only the scenes Quick was unsure of and says which pronouns are theirs. The rewrite stays Quick's.

## Talking to each other

Numbers › **Talking to each other** shows the scenes where two or more people in a group you choose (from the gender values you wrote, starting with the women) talk to each other: the first part of the Bechdel–Wallace test. In Studio, **What do they talk about** says in a few words what each conversation is about and whether its subject is a man. It is a floor, not a measure of quality, and it never says pass or fail.

## In Studio: the Assistant

The interview and the group interview speak from all of this, as part of a life rather than the topic, and each speaker knows only what they are named as knowing about the others. Scene features see how bodies and minds show for the people on the page; the continuity check knows pronouns and how a body shows. Whenever any of it is sent, rules go with it: describe only what you wrote, never invent a disability, illness, trauma or identity, and stay away from stock patterns (disability as tragedy or cure, inspiration, the queer character who dies for someone else's story, trauma as the reason for cruelty). An entry's ⋯ › **Keep out of the Assistant** leaves it out of every prompt.
