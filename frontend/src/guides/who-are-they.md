# Who are they

Write people with depth: who they are, how they think, their body and mind, and what formed them.

Open a character (logo menu › **Lorebook** › **Characters**) and choose **Who are they** beside **Overview**.

- **Every field is optional.** Nothing counts toward a complete sheet; empty fields wait in the **Add** row.
- **Nothing here is a flaw.** A disability, an illness, a gender or a history is not a fault of character.
- **Your words.** Identity-first ("autistic") or person-first ("has epilepsy") is your choice. Nothing is scored.

## Identity

Gender, how someone presents, sex and pronouns are four different things, so the sheet keeps them apart. Pronouns sit beside gender in the character form: the sheet's ⋯ › **Edit name, role, pronouns…**.

Some gender suggestions, such as two-spirit or fa'afafine, belong to a particular people and say where they come from. Each is a word of its own, not another way to say non-binary; if you use one, read about it from the people it belongs to.

## How they think and feel

How someone thinks, and what hurts them, shapes their voice.

- **How they think**, in words: "in pictures", "out loud".
- **How they think, temperament, sensitivity…** opens the scales. **Thinks** is how someone thinks, not how well; **Temperament** is how fast they boil, **Sensitivity** what reaches them.
- **How they take things**: what gets under their skin, how they cope, how long they hold on.

## Body and mind

Choose **Add to body and mind** for a disability, a long-term illness, neurodivergence or mental health. Pick the area, name it in your words, and write as much as you want:

- **How it shapes their days**: what they plan around, what it costs, what they are good at because of it.
- **What it does to them**: for anxiety, the thought that loops at 3 a.m., the friend who stopped being invited.
- **How it shows on the page**, and **What they'd want understood**.
- **Who knows**, and the scene where the reader learns it.

It is not a medical record: there is no severity scale or symptom list.

> **Tip:** The ordinary day says more than the crisis. Write what they plan around on a Tuesday.

## What formed them

**Add a formative experience**: what happened, what it did to them and how it shows. Adversity is broader than trauma (poverty, displacement, a public failure), and a good thing belongs here too.

- Tick **The wound behind their arc** on the one the story tests. The **Arc journey** tab then reads the arc in one line, from what formed them to what they learn.
- **Content notes, for you only** keep a hard entry folded until you choose **Show what happened**, so you never meet it unprepared.

## Goal, motivation and conflict

**Overview** opens on **The three questions**, from Debra Dixon: what do they want, why, and what stands in the way? Many writers add a fourth: what if they don't get it? A want, a reason and an obstacle give a character something to do in every scene.

**Needs** and **Believes** (the lie the story tests) follow. Plan › **The essentials** asks the same questions of your main characters.

## In the scene

The **Scene sheet**'s **Who, where, when** page shows how each person's body and mind show in that scene, from what you wrote. Hover an @mention in the prose to see their pronouns and what everyone in the story knows about them.

## Change pronouns or a name

1. Make the change in the sheet's ⋯ › **Edit name, role, pronouns…**.
2. **Review the manuscript** lists every sentence that should follow, marked **Sure** or **Unsure**.
3. Tick what should change and choose **Apply**, or **Skip** to change only the field.

Apply saves a version of the story first, and the whole change is one Undo. Check the unsure sentences yourself: the ⓘ beside **Quick, on this machine** lists what it can miss. Later, the sheet's ⋯ › **Review pronouns…** finds slips.

## Talking to each other

**Numbers** › **Talking to each other** shows the scenes where people from a group you choose, by the **Gender** values you wrote, talk to each other. It is the first part of the Bechdel–Wallace test: a floor, not a measure of quality, and it never says pass or fail.

## In Studio: the Assistant

The interview speaks from all of this, as part of a life rather than the topic, and each speaker knows only what **Who knows** allows. Whenever any of it is sent, rules go with it: describe only what you wrote, never invent a disability, illness, trauma or identity, and avoid stock patterns such as disability as tragedy or cure.

- Content notes are never sent. An entry's ⋯ › **Keep out of the Assistant** leaves it out of every prompt.
- In a pronoun review, **Careful, with the Assistant** rereads the scenes Quick was unsure of.
- On **Talking to each other**, **What do they talk about** says what each conversation is about.
