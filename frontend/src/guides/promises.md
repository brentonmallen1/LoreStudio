# Threads, twists and what the reader knows

Every story makes promises to the reader and then keeps them. A question raised on page one is a promise to answer it; a strange detail is a promise that it matters. Readers turn pages to see promises kept. **Promises**, in the story's menu, is where you plan them and check that each one pays off.

This guide uses _The Last Lighthouse_, the demo story, so you can open each thing it mentions.

## The tapestry

**Promises › The tapestry** draws every promise across the book in reading order: a row for each one, a column for each scene. A mark in a row is a scene doing something to that promise; click it to open the scene. Click a name to open its sheet. Planned scenes, with no prose yet, are in italics.

Below it, **Needs your eye** lists what may need a look: a thread that goes quiet, a clue that comes after its reveal. Each is a question, not a verdict. If it's deliberate, dismiss it.

## Threads: the questions a story keeps open

A **thread** is something the story opens and later closes. In _The Last Lighthouse_, _The Missing Logs_ opens in _The Light_ (why are six months of entries gone?) and closes in _The New Entry_, when Eleanor writes the gap down.

A thread is one of four kinds of promise (Mary Robinette Kowal calls them MICE):

- **A place**: it opens when we arrive somewhere and closes when we leave.
- **A question**: it opens with a question and closes with its answer.
- **A change**: it opens when someone is unhappy with who they are, and closes when they change, or refuse to.
- **A disruption**: it opens when the world is knocked out of order, and closes when a new order holds.

## A thread, scene by scene

A thread's sheet lists the scenes it runs through. For each one, say what the scene does to it:

- **opens it** and **closes it**: where the promise is made and kept.
- **moves it on**, **turns it** (somewhere the reader didn't expect) or **complicates it**.
- **A try**: someone tries to settle it. **A try fails**, **fails, worse**, **succeeds, at a cost** or **succeeds**. A run of failed tries before the end is what makes the ending land. _Eleanor's Father_ has three: she deflects, she finds the gap herself and it's worse, and at last she lets the false version of him go, at a cost.

Add a note on each scene in your own words. Scenes in between that leave the thread alone say **not here**, so a long silence shows.

A thread's status follows from its scenes: **planned** until it is in a scene, **open** once it is, **resolved** when a scene closes it. **Set it aside** if you have put it down for now; the checks then leave it alone.

## Threads together

Threads usually close in the reverse order they opened: the last question raised is the first answered, like nested brackets. _The Visitor's Identity_ opens and closes inside _The Missing Logs_, so they nest. When one opens inside another and closes after it, the sheet and the findings say so. Crossing on purpose is fine.

## Twists: a truth behind what the reader believes

A **twist** has two halves: **the truth**, and **what the reader is led to believe** until the reveal. In _Thomas Vance Falsified the Logs_, the reader believes the missing entries are a clerical gap; the truth is that Thomas destroyed them.

**Clues** are details that point **toward the truth** or **away from it**. Each has a subtlety, from obvious to hidden. The twist's sheet lays them out in reading order with the reveal at the end, and asks **Is it fair?**: could a careful reader get there before the reveal? Two quiet clues mean a careful reader could, and most won't. No clue toward the truth before the reveal feels like a cheat.

Plant a clue from the prose itself: select the words, choose **Plant a clue for…** in the toolbar, and pick the twist. The clue keeps the words. **Reveal a twist here…** marks the scene where the truth comes out.

Each twist has a colour, chosen with the diamond beside its name; a new twist starts on teal.

## Setups and payoffs

A setup is a smaller promise: a detail, a line or an image that a later scene pays off or returns to. **Promises › Setups and payoffs** lists every link between scenes as a sentence, earlier scene first: _The Light foreshadows The Gap_; _The Departure calls back to Knock at the Door_. Link two scenes there, or from either scene.

## What the reader knows

**Promises › What the reader knows** shows, scene by scene, what the reader learns, what they are led to believe and what only they know. Most of it fills itself in from your twists: a clue toward the truth is something learned, a clue away from it something believed, and a reveal teaches the truth and strikes out what the misdirection had them believe.

Add the rest under **Your entries**: what a character learns, and what **only the reader knows**. That last is dramatic irony. In _Knock at the Door_ the Visitor already knows Eleanor's name, and Eleanor lets it pass; the reader notices, and every quiet scene after that is tense.

## In the scene

The Scene sheet's **Promises** page is the scene's side of it: on the left, what this scene does to each thread it touches (a card each, with the role and a note) and the twists planted or revealed here; on the right, the threads open coming in and where each last moved, what the reader knows by the end, and the scenes linked to this one. Attach a thread there, or change what the scene does to it. The _This scene_ tab says it in a line while you write.
