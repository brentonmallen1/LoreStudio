# Threads, twists and what the reader knows

Plan the promises your story makes to the reader, and check each one is kept.

A question on page one promises an answer; a strange detail promises it matters. Readers turn pages to see promises kept. The examples here are from the demo story, _The Last Lighthouse_, so you can open each one.

## See every promise at once

Logo menu › **Promises** › **The tapestry** draws every thread, twist and setup in reading order: a row for each, a column for each scene. Click a mark to open the scene, or a name to open its sheet.

**Needs your eye**, below it, lists what may want a look, such as a thread that goes quiet. If it is deliberate, choose **It's intended** from the row's ⋯ menu.

## Threads: questions the story keeps open

A thread opens, and later closes. _The Missing Logs_ opens in _The Light_ (why are six months of entries gone?) and closes in _The New Entry_.

Add one with **New thread**. Its sheet's **What kind of promise** offers four shapes, from Mary Robinette Kowal's MICE:

- **A place**: opens when we arrive, closes when we leave.
- **A question**: opens with a question, closes with its answer.
- **A change**: opens when someone is unhappy with who they are, closes when they change, or refuse to.
- **A disruption**: opens when the world is knocked out of order, closes when a new order holds.

## Follow a thread scene by scene

Under **Scene by scene**, add each scene the thread runs through and pick what it does: **opens it**, **moves it on**, **turns it**, **complicates it**, a try that fails or succeeds, or **closes it**. A note in your words says how.

Failed tries raise the stakes, so the ending lands. _Eleanor's Father_ has three: she deflects, she finds the gap and it is worse, and at last she lets the false version of him go, at a cost.

- A scene that leaves the thread alone says **not here**, so a long silence shows.
- **With the other threads** says which threads nest. Threads usually close in the reverse order they opened, like brackets.
- **Set it aside** if you have put a thread down; the checks then leave it alone.

## Twists: a truth behind what the reader believes

A twist has two halves: **The truth**, and **What the reader is led to believe** until the reveal. In _Thomas Vance Falsified the Logs_, the reader thinks the missing entries are a clerical gap; in truth, Thomas destroyed them.

1. Add one under **Promises** › **Twists** and write both halves.
2. Under **When it lands**, choose the scene it is **Revealed in**.
3. Add clues under **Clues, in reading order**: each points **Toward the truth** or **Away from it**, from obvious to hidden.

**Is it fair?** says whether a careful reader could get there first. One quiet clue before the reveal makes it feel earned rather than sprung.

> **Tip:** Plant a clue as you write. Select the words, choose **Plant a clue for…** in the toolbar, and pick the twist. **Reveal a twist here…** marks where the truth comes out.

## Setups and payoffs

A setup is a smaller promise: a detail or an image a later scene pays off. **Promises** › **Setups and payoffs** lists each link as a sentence, such as _The Light foreshadows The Gap_. Link two scenes there, or from either scene's sheet.

## What the reader knows

**Promises** › **What the reader knows** shows, scene by scene, what the reader learns, what they are led to believe and what only they know. Your clues and reveals fill most of it in.

Add the rest under **Your entries**. What only the reader knows is dramatic irony: in _Knock at the Door_ the Visitor already knows Eleanor's name, and Eleanor lets it pass. The reader notices, and every quiet scene after is tense.

## In the scene

The **Scene sheet**'s **Promises** page shows what this scene does to each thread, the twists here, what is open coming in and what the reader knows by the end. Attach a thread there. While you write, **This scene** in the side panel sums it up in a line.
